The part that gets skipped

The technology lands. The strategy lands. The people are the variable nobody resourced.

Change rarely fails in the plan. It fails in the eight weeks either side of go-live, in the gap between what an executive team decided in March and what a shift supervisor understands on a Tuesday in October.

That gap is not a mystery. It is a resourcing decision. The people work is genuinely large. Done properly it means naming every group the change touches, sizing the impact on each one, measuring where they stand before you move, drafting comms that answer the question they are actually asking rather than the one you wish they were asking, and reporting all of it upward in a form a steering committee will accept.

That is six weeks of senior practitioner time. Most programmes do not have six weeks spare, so they produce the deck and quietly skip the rest. Then everyone is surprised when adoption is the problem.

The Foundry exists so the human side is not the part that gets skipped.

The part most tools get wrong

Written in their words, not in yours.

Most change comms describe the change from the project's point of view, because the project is who wrote them. The reader is then asked to work out what it means for their Tuesday. Most of them will not bother, and the ones who do will get it wrong.

What the project writes

“We are implementing an integrated cloud ERP with a digital rostering module, delivering a single source of truth across production, stock and finance.”

Accurate, approved, and completely useless to the person it lands on. It describes the system. It does not describe their day. It also quietly signals that nobody writing it has met them.

What they need to hear

“From 3 March your roster comes to your phone two weeks ahead instead of going up on the board on Friday. You will clock on at the tablet by the door. Your pay should stop needing corrections. For the first fortnight Sam is on the floor every shift if it goes wrong.”

Same change. Written in the language of the person it happens to, describing what changes in their day, when, and who to find when it breaks.

How it gets thereThe words come from them, not from us.

The impact register is built from interviews, then the comms are built from the register.

The Foundry generates an interview script for each impacted group and captures the conversation. It will also read your as-is and to-be documents and draft the impacts it finds between them. What goes into the register is what the people said, in the terms they used, about the parts of the day that actually change. Every message is then drafted from that register rather than from the project's description of itself.

Why it mattersGuessing is expensive.

You end up answering the worry you assumed, not the worry they have.

Programmes routinely spend a comms cycle reassuring people about job security when the actual fear was the Thursday night shift handover, or the fact that the new system needs an email address half the workforce does not have. Answering the wrong question costs you the cycle, and it costs you the credibility of the next one.
