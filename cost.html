The 60/40 problem · The cost of change busywork

You are paying change people to format.

Around sixty per cent of a change manager's time goes on producing documents. Strategy papers, stakeholder matrices, comms drafts, survey forms and the SteerCo pack, built and rebuilt every time the scope moves. Which leaves forty per cent for the only part that actually changes behaviour: being with the people the change lands on.

You are not underfunding change. You are funding the wrong sixty per cent.

Where the time actually goes

Sixty per cent of your change budget never touches a human being.

This is not a criticism of change managers. It is a description of the job as it is currently resourced. The artifacts are mandatory, the deadlines are real, and somebody has to build them. So the documents get done and the conversations get squeezed.

The second number is the one that matters, and it is the one nobody puts on a slide. The change team cost is visible and gets argued about in budget meetings. The productivity dip is invisible, lands on the operating line rather than the project line, and is several times larger.

The assumptions, written down. The sixty per cent figure is my own observation from two decades inside transformations, not a vendor statistic. Working year is taken as 220 days for cost purposes and 260 calendar weekdays for the productivity calculation. The recovery figure is set at three quarters rather than all of it, because reviewing and editing a generated document still takes real time and should. The productivity dip is applied evenly across everyone impacted, which is generous to the optimistic case, since in practice one or two cohorts carry most of it. If any of that is wrong for your situation, change the inputs or tell me and I will change the model.

What you get back

What the forty per cent is actually for.

Give a good change lead their time back and this is where it goes. Every one of these is a thing a person has to do. None of them can be generated, and none of them happen while somebody is reformatting a deck.

Gets the time backThe project

Someone is finally watching the humans, not just the plan.

Delivery risk and people risk are different risks and they surface in different rooms. When the change lead is in the delivery stand-up instead of in a document, the people risk gets named while there is still time to do something about it.

Gets the time backBehaviour change

Behaviour changes in conversations, not in comms.

Nobody works differently because they received an email. They work differently because their supervisor stood next to them and did it the new way, twice, on a bad day. That takes a person, on the floor, on the shift that actually runs at three in the morning.

Gets the time backTraining

Training aimed at the real gap, not the assumed one.

Most training is built from the system, so it teaches the software. Training built from a real impact register teaches the fifteen minutes of someone's day that genuinely change. It is shorter, it is cheaper, and people turn up.

Gets the time backStakeholder analysis

A stakeholder map is only worth the conversations it triggers.

The grid is not the work. The work is the sponsor sitting down with the high-influence detractor and asking what they are actually worried about. The Foundry produces the grid and tells you who to see first. You still have to go and see them, and now you have the afternoon to do it.

Gets the time backThe people themselves

Interviews. Actual ones, with the people it lands on.

This is the single highest-value hour in change work and it is the first thing cut when the deck is due. It is also where the words come from, which is the next section.
